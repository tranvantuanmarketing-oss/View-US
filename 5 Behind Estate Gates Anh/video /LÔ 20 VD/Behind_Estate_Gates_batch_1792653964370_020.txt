Title: Too Thick to Understand
Author: Karen Spencer
Content:
I signed the bank papers at the kitchen table because Graham said I was too thick to understand them.
When his business collapsed, he sneered: “You signed it, Karen, so our house is gone and it's your fault.”
Twenty years I kept his accounts for nothing, and the bank's letter called me an informed, willing guarantor.
At the possession hearing I asked one question: did the bank ever make sure I received independent legal advice?
The bank's barrister went quiet, because the solicitor's certificate they relied on carried a signature dated after the solicitor's death...

The bank's barrister, a smooth man called Mr Faulkner, shuffled through his bundle for a long minute. Then he whispered to his instructing solicitor. Then he asked the judge for a short adjournment.

My name is Karen Spencer. I'm forty-four, a part-time teaching assistant, and I live in Preston, in a three-bedroom semi that my husband Graham has just lost to the bank.

Graham ran a groundworks company. For twenty years I did his books at the kitchen table, in the evenings, after the children were in bed. I never took a salary. I thought of it as our business, because everything we had was riding on it.

Four years ago, he told me the firm needed a bigger loan to buy new machinery. The bank wanted security. Our house, the only thing we owned, was to be charged as security for the company's debts. And I had to sign.

I asked to read the documents. Graham sighed. He said it was boring, that I wouldn't follow it, and that the bank had “everything in hand”. He said I was too thick for finance. I was embarrassed, so I signed. A bank manager called Mr Pritchard smiled and shook my hand.

When the pandemic hit, Graham's firm went under. The bank sought possession of our house. I was served with a court claim. The letter called me a fully informed guarantor. Graham, meanwhile, told his friends it was my fault for signing.

My solicitor, Mrs Rahman, was calm. She told me about a case called Royal Bank of Scotland against Etridge. In England and Wales, when a wife stands surety for her husband's debts, the bank is on notice that undue influence may be present. To protect itself, it must take reasonable steps to make sure she's received independent legal advice from a solicitor, who confirms in writing that she understood the risk.

In my case, the bank relied on a certificate. It said I'd been advised by a solicitor named Mr Alderson of Chorley. I had never heard of him. I'd never met anyone.

At the hearing, I asked the question. And the bank's barrister could not answer, because when Mrs Rahman had looked into it, she found something she could scarcely believe. Mr Alderson had died on the fourteenth of March. His certificate was dated the ninth of June.

The judge adjourned and told the bank to explain. That night, I felt weirdly calm.

Then, a week later, a courier delivered a small padded envelope. No note. Inside was a USB stick. I plugged it into my daughter's laptop with trembling hands.

There were nine voice recordings. In the first, my husband's voice was talking to someone about my signature. The second voice was Mr Pritchard, the bank manager. He was laughing, easy and relaxed. “Don't worry, Graham,” he said. “Nobody ever checks the certificates...”
