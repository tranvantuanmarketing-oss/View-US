Title: Stealing My Grass
Author: Dennis Pike
Content:
I grazed ponies in the paddock behind my Dorset cottage for fourteen years, mending every fence at my own expense.
Neighbouring landowner Hector Strand-Lyle barked in the village pub: “You've been stealing my grass, Pike, like a common thief.”
Everyone heard him call me a thief, though he had not set foot on that land once in fourteen years.
I finished my pint, went home, and took out the Land Registry application I had prepared months earlier.
Hector's solicitor wrote back, and one sentence about who actually owned the paddock made my hands shake...

PART 2:

I read the sentence three times standing at my kitchen counter. Then I sat down on the cold flagstones, because my legs had stopped working.

My name is Dennis Pike. I'm sixty-four, a retired farrier, and I live in a stone cottage on the edge of a village near Dorchester.

Fourteen years ago, I moved the last of my wife's two old ponies into the paddock behind my cottage. It was a rough acre of grass, bounded by a hawthorn hedge, and nobody had touched it for years. I asked around. Nobody knew who owned it. The old farmer who'd used it had died, his land had been sold in a jumble, and the paddock had fallen through the cracks.

So I mended the fences. I cleared the brambles. I put in a gate. I mowed, I grazed, I paid the vet. I locked the gate every night. As far as anyone could tell, it was mine.

Hector Strand-Lyle owns three hundred acres of rolling farmland that border mine on two sides. He's a tall, red-faced man who wears his tweed as if it's armour. He has never, in fourteen years, so much as waved at me.

Last winter, he walked into the Fox and Hounds and told the whole pub that I'd been “stealing his grass” for years. He said it was only because I was “an old man with ponies” that he hadn't called the police.

I went home and thought about it. Actually, I'd been thinking about it for a long time. My nephew, a surveyor, had explained adverse possession to me the year before. If you have occupied registered land, openly and without permission, for at least ten years, you can apply to the Land Registry to be registered as its owner. The registered owner is notified, and has sixty-five days to object. If they do, they must then take action to evict you within two years, or you win.

I'd filled in the application months earlier but never posted it. After Hector's performance, I posted it the next morning.

A fortnight later, a letter arrived from Hector's solicitors. It was a sneering, pompous thing. I braced myself for threats. Instead, it said, quite flatly, that their client did not own the paddock, and never had.

It said the Land Registry's records showed the registered owner, and that they were writing out of “courtesy”.

The name on the title was not Hector's. It was the name on my wedding ring's inscription. Rosemary Pike. My wife. Who died in 2009, three years before I ever moved a pony into that field.

Rosemary never told me she bought it. And when I rang the Land Registry, the clerk said there was a document lodged with the title that had been waiting a very long time for someone to read it...
